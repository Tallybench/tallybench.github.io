How to set your freelance hourly rate

Don't copy someone else's rate. Work backwards from the income you need and the hours you can really bill.

Why "salary ÷ 2,080" is too low

A full-time job pays you for about 2,080 hours a year (40 hours × 52 weeks), including holidays and sick days, and your employer covers equipment, software and part of your taxes. As a freelancer you pay for all of that yourself, and you can only invoice for some of your working time. Dividing a salary by 2,080 gives a rate that quietly leaves out all of those costs.

The five steps

  1. Decide your target take-home. This is what you want to keep each year after expenses and tax.
  2. Add your business expenses. Software, equipment, insurance, accounting, marketing, a share of your internet and phone, and so on.
  3. Add a tax set-aside. Your rate has to cover tax too. Use a percentage you've confirmed with an accountant or your tax authority's guidance. A simple way to estimate: revenue needed = (take-home + expenses) ÷ (1 − tax %).
  4. Count your billable hours. Start from 52 weeks, subtract holidays, vacation and sick time, multiply by your working hours per week, then multiply by the share of that time you can actually bill. Finding clients, quoting, invoicing and learning all take unbilled time, so for most solo workers this share is well below 100%. Track your own hours for a few weeks to find yours.
  5. Divide. Revenue needed ÷ billable hours = your minimum hourly rate.

A worked example

  • Target take-home: $60,000
  • Business expenses: $6,000
  • Tax set-aside: 25%
  • Time off: 5 weeks, working 40 hours a week
  • Billable share: 60%

Revenue needed = ($60,000 + $6,000) ÷ 0.75 = $88,000. Billable hours = 47 weeks × 40 hours × 60% = 1,128 hours. Hourly rate = $88,000 ÷ 1,128 ≈ $78.01.

Notice how much the billable share matters. At 80% billable the same numbers give about $58.51 an hour; at 40%, about $117.02. If you're unsure, run the numbers with a few different values in the hourly rate calculator.

What to do with the number

  • Treat it as a floor. It's the minimum that meets your goals, not what the market will bear. Many clients will pay more for specialised skills.
  • Consider project pricing. Quote a fixed price built from your estimated hours at this rate, plus a buffer for revisions. If you work faster than expected, you keep the difference.
  • Review it yearly. Costs change, and so should your rate. Raise it for new clients first.
  • Sanity-check the markup on materials. If you resell parts or supplies, price those separately with the markup and margin calculator. See also markup vs margin.
